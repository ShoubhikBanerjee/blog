# AI Models Help Independent Researchers Identify New Exoplanet Candidates

Independent researchers have used AI coding and analysis tools to identify new planetary signals in data from NASA's Kepler and TESS missions, contributing to the growing catalog of over 6,400 confirmed exoplanets.

## 🧩 How it works
Two independent efforts leveraged AI to process large datasets:

*   **Kevin Bond** used Codex to search observations of about 100,000 stars from the Kepler telescope. Codex compared different search methods, combined them into a single program, and tested code changes to improve results. When run on public Kepler data, the program flagged more than 200 signals, including 10 from previously confirmed planets.
*   **Ethan Byrd**, founder of the email infrastructure start-up Primitive, used a model called Astra to design two searches analyzing data from TESS and other public telescope archives. His searches identified 49 candidates in the first pass.

## ⚙️ Key details
The search produced specific planetary candidates that are now undergoing independent verification:

| Star | Signal Period | Estimated Size/Type | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| KIC 5689121 | 13.8 days | ~2.4x Earth's diameter | Cool star, half the size of the sun, in Lyra |
| KIC 6691030 | 4.8 days | Smaller than Neptune | Faint star in the northern sky |

An astronomer at the SETI Institute independently found two signals in the Kepler data that matched Bond's findings. Byrd has sent one of his strongest TESS candidates to NASA researchers for further analysis, with two more candidates awaiting submission.

## 💡 Why it matters
The 2017 success of neural networks finding an eighth planet orbiting Kepler-90 set a precedent for AI-assisted astronomy. These new efforts extend that approach, using large language models to write and optimize code that sifting through millions of stellar observations to find previously hidden planetary transits.
