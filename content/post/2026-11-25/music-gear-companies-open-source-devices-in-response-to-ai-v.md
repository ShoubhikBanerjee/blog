# Music gear companies open-source devices in response to AI vibe-coding

The emergence of powerful vibe-coding agents and tools has created chaos in the music gear world, forcing companies to determine how to respond to a wave of AI-aided custom firmwares and modding communities. While some manufacturers have remained silent, others have decided to embrace the trend by opening up their devices and providing tools for the community.

## 🔍 Overview

As it became clear that no piece of music gear was safe from AI tools and curious tinkerers, companies began making their devices open source. Major names like inMusic, the parent company of Akai, Moog, and Native Instruments, decided to "join 'em" rather than resist. Meanwhile, companies like Roland, Korg, Arturia, and Novation have yet to issue public statements on the matter.

## ⚙️ Key details

- **inMusic**: inMusic CEO Jack O’Donnell stated the company will provide a toolbox and the inner workings of devices like the MPC or Sample so people can do what they want. This includes a recently launched SDK and several open-source devices, including the recently teased MPC Sample Key. Akai aims to walk a fine line by offering modders access while keeping products reliable for professionals, promising a "reset button" so users can experiment without losing their original setup.
- **Behringer**: The company announced a slew of open-source devices, including a $150 clone of the MPC One called Kick, a clone of the E-mu SP-1200, the Anima DJ controller, the Ignite sequencer, and the Polykeys 61 MIDI controller. Launch dates for these products are currently unclear.
- **M-Vave**: The company’s $70 pocketable clone of the Yamaha DX7 became popular due to its price and hackability, leading to more than a dozen custom firmwares. M-Vave responded by releasing an open-source firmware for developers and confirmed its successor, the FM-2, will also be open source.
- **Erica Synths**: The company released the source code for its LXR-02 drum machine. Founder Ģirts Ozoliņš noted the sustainability of using one device with various options instead of buying multiple "plastic boxes," while acknowledging that vibe coding does not offset the environmental impact of data centers.
- **Polyend**: Open-sourced its line of Tracker grooveboxes and released the Endless guitar pedal last year, which allows users to vibe-code their own effects.
- **Elektron**: The company banned links to custom firmware from its official forums, warning that modifications could brick machines, prevent updates, or affect warranty coverage. CPO Christian Alsing stated that while he is sympathetic to the hacker community and believes users have the right to modify their devices, he pushed back on accusations that the company is withholding features.

## 💡 Why it matters

The shift toward open-sourcing hardware and firmware reflects a strategic pivot in the music technology industry. Companies are balancing the potential for community innovation and sustainability against the risks of hardware instability and the environmental costs associated with AI development.
