# European Energy Sector Updates Cybersecurity Strategies to Address AI-Driven Grid Threats

Experts gathering at the 9th Cybersecurity Forum in Brussels are warning that the European energy sector must close the gap between rapidly evolving cyber threats and practical response capabilities. The forum addresses new risks created by geopolitical attacks, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into malicious operations, and the growth of connected energy technologies.

## 🔍 Overview
The event follows the release of ENISA’s Threat Landscape 2026, which analyzed 8,257 cyber incidents from 2025. The report found that attackers are increasingly incorporating AI into their operations while claims of intrusions related to operational technology (OT)—the systems used to control physical equipment and industrial processes—have increased.

## 🧩 How it works
The sector is observing how AI can be used as both a tool for attackers and a critical component of grid defense. 

| Entity | AI and Cybersecurity Application |
| :--- | :--- |
| NPC Ukrenergo | Uses AI to help cybersecurity teams react faster, identify gaps, support staff, and assist with reporting |
| Malicious Actors | Incorporate AI into operations to shape geopolitical cyber activity and target OT systems |
| ENCS | Identifies vulnerabilities in connected devices that use unauthenticated control protocols |

## ⚙️ Key details
Recent real-world attacks provide a baseline for what grid cyber resilience requires in practice:
* **Poland (December 2025):** A coordinated attack targeted wind and solar farms and a combined heat and power plant. Although electricity generation continued, attackers disrupted communications and gained access levels that could have enabled further disruption.
* **Ukraine:** NPC Ukrenergo is sharing lessons on maintaining a grid under sustained attack, emphasizing the challenge of keeping specialist cybersecurity expertise when skilled teams are difficult to rebuild.
* **Connected Device Risks:** ENCS researchers demonstrated that attackers can use compromised everyday devices, such as routers or cameras, to communicate with residential solar inverters or home batteries that lack command authentication.

## 💡 Why it matters
The expansion of high-power IoT devices creates a risk where manipulating thousands of units simultaneously could cause sudden changes in generation or demand, potentially leading to wider grid disturbances. Harm van den Brink, a security researcher at ENCS, noted that vulnerabilities in these devices have already been exploited and requires a shift toward independent security testing and manufacturer-led vulnerability management. Dimitra Liveri of ENISA stated that the challenge requires moving from protecting individual systems to optimizing the entire ecosystem for resilience.
