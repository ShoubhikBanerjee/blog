# Meta removes AI training language from commercial terms

Meta has revised its commercial terms to strip out wording that had been read as granting the company permission to scrape content—including news sites—for AI training.

## 🔍 Overview
- The update eliminates the specific clause that could be interpreted as allowing Meta to use any content from domains, web pages, or URLs for developing and training its AI products.
- The change follows backlash from experts and content‑owners.

## ✍️ What changed
- The controversial language no longer appears on the commercial terms page.
- The removed clause previously read:
  > “… Meta may use any content from such domains, web pages, or URLs to provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity of, the Meta Company Products, services, and features …”
- Meta states that the crawling terms now apply only to its ads and business products.

## 🗣️ Company response
- Andy Stone, Meta’s vice president of communications, and spokesperson Daniel Roberts said, “There is no change to how Meta uses web crawlers; any suggestion to the contrary is false.”
- Roberts added in a follow‑up statement to The Verge:
  > “We’ve heard you and want to clarify: the crawling terms we consolidated only applied to our ads and business products, and we’ve clarified that in the terms themselves. It does not change how Meta uses web crawlers. As always, content owners can use robots.txt to signal their preferences. This is not about scraping news sites, and these updates don’t give us new rights that supersede those preferences.”

## 📜 Expert interpretation
- Neiman Lab and James Grimmelmann, professor of digital and information law at Cornell, had interpreted the original wording as an attempt to give Meta the right to scrape sites for free and to override a site’s robots.txt instructions.

## ⚖️ Why it matters
- Removing the language addresses concerns that Meta could train AI models on scraped content without compensation to content owners.
- The clarification reaffirms that standard web‑crawling behavior and robots.txt signals remain unchanged.
- It highlights ongoing tension between large tech platforms’ AI ambitions and existing copyright and web‑scraping norms.
