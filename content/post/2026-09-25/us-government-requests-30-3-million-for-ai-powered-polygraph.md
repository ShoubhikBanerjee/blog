# US Government Requests $30.3 Million for AI-Powered Polygraph Modernization

The US government has requested $30.3 million over the next five years to develop an improved form of lie detector known as Polygraph+ (or Polygraph Next). The program aims to modernize federal credibility assessment technologies to enhance accuracy and reliability.

## 🔍 Overview
Managed by the Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency (DCSA), Polygraph+ will be utilized for the vetting of prospective employees and "insider threat detection." This comes as the Pentagon has increasingly used polygraph tests under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to identify sources of press leaks, including the testing of approximately 50 Joint Staff officers following reports on US weapons stockpiles in the war with Iran.

## 🧩 How it works
The program focuses on two primary technical advancements:
* **AI and Machine Learning:** The use of scoring algorithms to find patterns in data that human examiners may miss and to create "multi-modal" deception detection by combining multiple measurements into a single score.
* **Standoff Sensing:** The ability to take physiological readings without attaching a device to the subject.

## ⚙️ Key details
Following an open submission process by the Defense Innovation Unit (DIU) in 2023, two companies were selected to build prototypes:

| Company | Capability/Focus |
| :--- | :--- |
| Presage Technologies | Measuring heart rate and breathing rate using standard cameras |
| Altec Research | Non-contact sensing tracking head movement, facial skin temperature, and pore activity |

## 💡 Why it matters
Current polygraph technology, largely unchanged since the 1920s, relies on measurements of sweat, breathing, pulse, and blood pressure. However, the technology faces significant criticism:
* **Reliability Issues:** In 1983, Congress’s Office of Technology Assessment found limited evidence supporting its use for employee screening, and in 2003, the US National Research Council called the evidence for its efficacy "weak at best."
* **Subjectivity and Bias:** Interpretations can vary wildly between examiners, and individuals from minority groups are more likely to be judged as deceptive.
* **Countermeasures:** Interviewees can beat the test through training, such as using a pin in a shoe to artificially heighten physiological responses.
* **Scale Risks:** With 2.8 million employees in the DOD, an imperfect system could result in tens of thousands of false accusations.

Critics such as legal scholar Kyri Kotsoglou describe the effort as "a misguided effort to reduce the complex to something that is tangible," while associate professor Sophie van der Zee notes that while various strategies like brain scans and thermal cameras have been tried, "There is still no Pinocchio’s nose."
