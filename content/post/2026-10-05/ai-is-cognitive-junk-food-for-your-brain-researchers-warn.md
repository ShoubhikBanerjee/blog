# AI Is Cognitive Junk Food For Your Brain, Researchers Warn

A new analysis argues that artificial intelligence functions as a cognitive version of a hot dog, appealing in the moment but ultimately bad for cognitive health. The warning is grounded in neuroscience and evolutionary history, challenging the prevalent idea that the human brain is simply a biological computer.

## 🧩 Overview

The argument begins with a fundamental critique of how the AI industry views the human mind. The evidence suggests that while influential figures like Alan Turing, Demis Hassabis, and Elon Musk have promoted the idea that the brain is a computer, this model is dangerously incomplete.

The evidence quotes a central claim: "AI, it turns out, is something like a cognitive version of a hot dog: It appeals to us in the moment, but undermines the health and sustainability of cognitive systems we’ve evolved over several millennia."

## 🔍 How It Works

The problematic "brain as computer" model posits thought as a three-step algorithm:

1. **Input**: The mind takes in information from the world.
2. **Computation**: It manipulates that information.
3. **Output**: It enacts behavior.

While this model has been productive in many ways, critics—including John von Neumann—doubted it could capture the "exceptional complexity of the human nervous system."

An alternative model, championed by researcher Cisek, suggests that brains are better understood as feedback-control systems, not information processors. This is not a new idea: philosopher John Dewey described the mind as a circuit where "the motor response determines the stimulus, just as truly as sensory stimulus determines movement."

| Model | Core Idea | Proponents / Critics |
|-------|-----------|---------------------|
| Brain as computer | Input, computation, output algorithm | Alan Turing, Demis Hassabis, Elon Musk |
| Brain as feedback-control system | Minds take action to adjust the stimulus they receive | Cisek, John Dewey |

A classic example from baseball illustrates the difference. To catch a fly ball, the feedback-control approach suggests a simple heuristic: "keep the ball in the same position within your visual field, and then move to maintain that situation."

## 💡 Why It Matters

The evolutionary history of the nervous system supports the feedback-control view. The evidence states: "From ancient fish to amphibians to mammals to primates and eventually modern humans, what we observe is that new behaviors emerge in response to new environmental possibilities." The nervous system's history is described as one of "continuous extension of control further and further into the world."

Norbert Wiener, godfather of cybernetics, is quoted: "The thought of every age is reflected in its technique." The warning is that the current age's technique—AI—may be reducing the cognitive complexity humans have evolved over millennia. As the evidence puts it, "humans are more complex than a straightforward comparison to computers gives us credit for — and far more than most of the AI industry seems to appreciate."
