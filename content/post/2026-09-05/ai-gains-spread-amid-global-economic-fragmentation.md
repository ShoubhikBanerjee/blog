# AI Gains Spread Amid Global Economic Fragmentation

New research shows that AI breakthroughs remain clustered in a few economies, yet the productivity gains can diffuse worldwide; the extent of that diffusion depends on how fragmented the global economy has become.

## 🔍 Overview
- Artificial intelligence is advancing at the same time as the global economy is becoming more fragmented.
- AI innovation is concentrated in a small number of economies, while the gains from AI depend on technologies, intermediate inputs, data, investment, and know‑how moving across borders.
- Fragmentation can therefore shape not only the size of the global gains from AI, but also who receives them.

## 🧩 Layers of the AI ecosystem
| Layer | Typical participants | Geographic concentration |
|-------|----------------------|---------------------------|
| Frontier innovators | Entities that create frontier AI models | Highly concentrated, especially in the US, China, and the EU |
| Supply‑chain and infrastructure providers | Producers of semiconductors, cloud services, AI‑enabled machinery, etc. | Much broader |
| Adopters | Firms that use AI‑related hardware, software, services, or AI‑enabled intermediate inputs | Much broader |

## ⚙️ Diffusion channel and model results
- Firms can become more productive without developing AI themselves: they can use better semiconductors, software, cloud services, or AI‑enabled machinery produced elsewhere.
- Our benchmark simulation using the IMF’s ECLIPSSE model illustrates the size of this diffusion channel.
- When AI raises productivity at the frontier and also improves the efficiency with which AI‑related intermediate inputs are used elsewhere, modeled global GDP is 1.64 % above the baseline.
- The gains are 2.28 % in the US, 1.87 % in China, 1.93 % in the EU and 0.94 % in the rest of the world.
- These numbers are scenario‑based model estimates, not forecasts. Their purpose is to show how gains propagate through production networks.

## 📉 Impact of economic fragmentation
- Barriers to trade and technology diffusion – tariffs, export controls, investment screening, restrictions on data flows, and incompatible digital regulations – can make AI‑related inputs more costly or less accessible.
- These channels are part of the broader costs of geoeconomic fragmentation documented by Aiyar et al. (2023).
- Fragmentation can also slow innovation at the frontier itself by reducing collaboration, knowledge flows, market scale and access to complementary inputs.
- In our simulations, trade fragmentation alone reduces global GDP gains from 1.64 % to 1.49 %; the rest‑of‑world gain slips from 0.94 % to 0.93 %.
- When fragmentation also slows frontier AI progress, global gains fall further to 1.29 % and rest‑of‑world gains to 0.85 %.
- It is that fragmentation damages the innovation process that creates technologies for everyone.

## 💡 Policy options for non‑frontier economies
- For economies not at the technological frontier, preserving diversified links may be a valuable form of economic optionality.
- The MENAP economies in our sample maintain significant trade and investment links with the US, Europe and China; that diversification can become an asset if geopolitical rivalry intensifies.
- Simulations compare ‘choosing sides’ with three more active strategies:
  1. **Connector strategy** – lowering lawful trade and regulatory frictions with partners on multiple sides – can more than offset the losses from fragmentation in our experiment.
  2. **Improving AI preparedness** – upgrading digital infrastructure, skills, and institutions to raise the capacity to absorb AI.
  3. **Strengthening local AI‑related capacity** – developing domestic software, data, adaptation, and deployment services to deepen domestic gains.
