# OpenAI safety chief resigns, warns of broken industry culture and need for nuclear‑level safeguards

David Robinson, the author of safety reports that accompanied every major model release at OpenAI, resigned this week and published an editorial in *The Atlantic* outlining his concerns about the company’s safety culture.

## 🔍 Overview
- Robinson wrote the safety reports for each major OpenAI model release.
- He resigned from his safety position at OpenAI.
- His resignation was accompanied by an editorial in *The Atlantic*.
- An OpenAI safety employee has quit and is sounding the alarm.

## ⚙️ Core concerns
- Robinson says the culture in industry is fundamentally broken.
- He describes Silicon Valley’s operating mindset as “extreme confidence” and “perpetual sprints,” building ever larger models with “unimpeded optimism” that ignores or underestimates potential problems.
- He argues the issue runs deeper than merely adding new rules or regulations.
- He calls for AI companies to develop humility and look outside the insular “move‑fast‑and‑break‑things” world of tech.
- He notes it is understandable to feel cynical about sudden warnings, but that should not cause us to discount them.

## 🛡️ Proposed safeguards
- Robinson says AI needs “nuclear‑level safeguards.”
- He likens frontier labs to nuclear power plants or busy airports, requiring layers of redundancy and careful, time‑consuming planning so that inevitable human error does not open a door to disaster.

## 📈 Industry exodus
- Robinson is the latest in a growing parade of researchers and safety workers who have left prominent AI firms.
- Notable recent departures include:
  - Jacob Coxon – Anthropic (publicly warned AI “could kill us all by the end of the decade.”)
  - Robert O’Callahan – Google DeepMind
  - Bilal Chughtai – Google DeepMind
  - Josh Engels – Google DeepMind
  - Joe Benton – Anthropic

## 💡 Why it matters
- The series of resignations underscores growing internal alarm about AI safety.
- Parallel concerns arise in cybersecurity: Microsoft’s Digital Defence Report 2026 notes a shift from AI assistants to semi‑autonomous attacks, with AI systems capable of leading or independently carrying out complex attack chains.
- In controlled tests, an advanced AI system passed 32 sequential stages of a complex attack, and the median time from vulnerability discovery to exploitation has fallen below 24 hours, while large companies often need 30‑60 days to remediate critical external vulnerabilities.
- These developments emphasize that safety warnings from insiders should be taken seriously and that robust, multi‑layered safeguards are increasingly urgent.
