# New TACLS Software Uses Machine Learning to Improve Flash Flood Alerts

Scientists from UCSD, the NWS, and NASA have collaborated to create the Transient Artifact and Continuous Learning System (TACLS), a new software designed to help the National Weather Service (NWS) identify areas at risk of flash flooding more quickly.

## 🧩 How it works
TACLS utilizes a combination of satellites and machine learning to identify specific locations on a map that risk transitioning from rain to dangerous flash floods. This system aims to assist the 122 NWS weather forecast offices throughout the US and its territories in making better decisions when issuing flash flood alerts.

## ⚙️ Key details
NWS offices currently track storms using weather radars and satellites, and rely on stream and rain gauges to monitor water levels. However, these systems face certain limitations:
* Deserts and less populated areas typically have fewer rain gauges.
* NWS satellites provide more detail over oceans than over land.

## 💡 Why it matters
Floods are the deadliest type of weather event globally and the second-deadliest in the United States. The danger is significant based on water depth:
* 6 inches: Can knock an adult off their feet.
* 12 inches: Can lift a car.
* 2 feet: Can move larger vehicles such as SUVs and trucks.

The NWS distinguishes a flash flood from a regular flood based on time; if an area floods in under six hours, it is classified as a flash flood.

## ⚠️ Alert Levels

| Alert Type | Severity | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Watch | Least serious | Issued 12 to 48 hours in advance |
| Advisory | Moderate | Nuisance-level flooding that does not threaten life or property |
| Warning | Most serious | Highest level of alert |

## 🚀 Availability
TACLS is currently used to identify risk areas in California, but the technology will soon be available to every NWS office.
