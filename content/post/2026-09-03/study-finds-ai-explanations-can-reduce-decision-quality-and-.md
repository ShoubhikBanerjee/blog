# Study Finds AI Explanations Can Reduce Decision Quality and Increase False Negatives

New research from Harvard Business School, MIT and the University of Washington shows that AI systems that provide narrative explanations can lead evaluators to make poorer decisions than plain recommendations.

## 🔍 Overview
- Researchers examined how 228 experienced evaluators screened real submissions to a global social‑impact challenge (MIT Solve).
- Three conditions were compared: human‑only evaluation, black‑box LLM recommendations, and LLM recommendations with narrative explanations.
- The study was motivated by the growing use of AI tools in Australia and New Zealand for funding, hiring, risk assessment and other high‑stakes decisions, many of which advertise built‑in explanations as a safeguard.

## 🧪 Study Design
- Evaluators reviewed innovation proposals that had also been judged by an independent expert panel.
- The AI tool provided either:
  1. A recommendation only (black‑box).
  2. The same recommendation plus a generated narrative explanation.
- Researchers measured compliance (how often evaluators followed the AI) and decision quality (agreement with the expert panel).

## 📊 Findings
- **Black‑box recommendations improve decision quality.**
- **Narrative explanations increase compliance but do not improve outcomes.**
  - “We find that black‑box recommendations … improve decision quality, whereas narrative explanations do not, despite inducing higher compliance.”
- Adding explanations pushed evaluators to defer further to the AI, even when the recommendation was wrong.
- Narrative explanations created an *asymmetric compliance* effect:
  - Evaluators were especially likely to follow AI suggestions to **reject** ideas.
  - This led to a substantial rise in *false negatives* – proposals rejected by evaluators that the expert panel believed should advance.
- Explanations suppressed “productive overrides,” where humans would otherwise reject a flawed AI recommendation.
  - “Mechanism analyses show that narratives suppress productive overrides by substituting persuasive text for independent verification.”
- The explanations were designed for linguistic fluency and persuasiveness, not for exposing the underlying logic, creating an “illusion of explanatory depth.”

## ⚖️ Implications
- Explainability, while a regulatory and vendor priority, may increase trust **without** increasing accuracy.
- “A convincing explanation is not necessarily evidence that a recommendation is correct.”
- Policymakers and vendors should distinguish between truly transparent explanations and persuasive narratives that can mislead decision‑makers.
- Organizations deploying AI for funding, hiring, risk assessment, etc., may need to reconsider reliance on narrative explanations and encourage productive overrides.

## 🚀 Why it matters
- As AI tools expand into high‑impact domains, ensuring that explanations genuinely aid judgment—not just persuade—is critical to avoid overlooking valuable innovations and other worthwhile opportunities.
