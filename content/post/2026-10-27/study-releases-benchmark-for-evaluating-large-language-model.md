# Study Releases Benchmark for Evaluating Large Language Model Novelty Judgments

A new study titled **Old Ideas, Novel Problems** was released, investigating how reliably large language models can evaluate the novelty of scientific research ideas. The authors also published a benchmark, accompanying data, and code for testing novelty judges, together with an automated pipeline for creating new evaluation sets.

## 🔍 Overview
- The study introduces **novelty judges**, which assess whether a research idea is new relative to prior work, either on its own or compared with another idea.
- Six LLM judges were evaluated: GPT‑5.1, GPT‑5.2, GPT‑5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Opus 4.5, and Claude Opus 4.6.
- Two toolkits were released to support researchers:
  - **DataVoyager** – helps explore and analyze structured data.
  - **AutoDiscovery** – generates and tests hypotheses.
  - **Theorizer** – synthesizes evidence across large literature bodies into candidate scientific theories.

## 🧩 How it Works
- An automated pipeline was built around **ICLR 2026** peer reviews.
- Review passages mentioning originality were mined instead of using paper acceptance as a proxy.
- **Human‑Only pool**: 154 high‑novelty ideas were selected from accepted papers in the top‑rated 10 % of their ICLR area where reviewers positively identified novelty and no reviewer disputed it.
- **Low‑novelty pool**: 145 ideas were taken from rejected papers in the bottom‑10 % where reviewers raised originality concerns without any positive novelty signal.
- **Human+Generated pool**: The 154 validated human ideas were paired with 154 ideas generated by a simple LLM generator that received only a research area and an instruction to propose a novel idea (no literature search, tools, iteration, or feedback).
- An independent expert reviewed 30 Human+Generated pairs (heavily weighted toward cases the strongest judge got wrong) and preferred the human idea in all 30 pairs, judging none of the generated ideas novel.

## ⚙️ Key Details
### Model Variants Tested
| Model | Variant |
|-------|---------|
| GPT   | 5.1 |
| GPT   | 5.2 |
| GPT   | 5.4 |
| Claude Sonnet | 4.5 |
| Claude Opus   | 4.5 |
| Claude Opus   | 4.6 |

- **Prompt sensitivity**: Changing just a few words in a prompt shifted a model’s accuracy in identifying which of two ideas is more novel by **more than 50 percentage points**.
- **Retrieval & reasoning**: Adding retrieval or additional reasoning steps did **not consistently improve** judgments.
- **Purpose‑built systems**: Two systems designed specifically for novelty evaluation performed **worse than a simple prompted Claude Opus 4.6 baseline**.
- **Label independence**: Many core findings hold regardless of the novelty labels; opposite answers from reasonable evaluation setups expose instability.
- **Evaluation modes**: Judges were tested on both **pointwise** (absolute novelty scores) and **pairwise** (comparing two ideas) tasks, varying one factor at a time—prompt wording, reasoning budget, access to related prior work, and output format (score vs. comparison).
- **Validation gap**: The judges are often validated only on human‑written papers, not on AI‑generated ideas they are meant to assess.
- **Acceptance ≠ novelty**: A paper can be accepted without being particularly novel if it is strong in other aspects.

## 🚀 Availability
- The benchmark, raw data, and evaluation code are publicly released alongside the paper.
- The automated pipeline for constructing new evaluation sets is also provided.

## 💡 Why it Matters
- The dramatic impact of minor prompt changes highlights **instability** in LLM‑based novelty assessment.
- Discrepancies between evaluation setups reveal that current novelty judges lack reliable, consistent criteria.
- Providing a public benchmark and pipeline enables the community to measure, compare, and improve novelty‑judging systems.
