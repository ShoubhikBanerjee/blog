# Microsoft Argues Copilot Chat Logs Show Rare Copyright Regurgitation in Lawsuit

Microsoft has filed new legal documents arguing that its Copilot chatbot rarely reproduces full sentences from news articles or books, asserting that virtually no users are obtaining copyrighted publisher content through the tool.

## ⚖️ Legal arguments and fair use

Microsoft submitted its filing on Friday as it argues for the judge to issue a summary judgment, which would end the case at an early stage. The company argues that the data bolsters its case that using copyrighted content for AI training datasets should be considered fair use. While systems like Copilot rely on using copyrighted material, Microsoft says the resulting systems are used for significantly different purposes than the original. The fact that they sometimes reproduce sections of text, the company concludes, "hardly undermines the transformative purpose of LLM training."

## 📊 Analysis of chat logs

As part of the lawsuit's discovery, Microsoft provided 8.2 million Copilot chat logs to an expert hired by news publishers. The logs were specifically chosen because they hit on keywords implicating the use of the publishers' websites, making them the most likely to contain the plaintiffs' works. According to Microsoft, the resulting analysis shows very low rates of reproduction:

* Fewer than 1 percent of the more than 8 million chat logs regurgitated at least 16 words.
* A total of 59,545 logs contained at least 16 words in common with news content used to ground the AI model.
* An expert for the Center for Investigative Reporting (CIR) found 51 instances of "substantial overlap" with CIR work in the dataset.
* An expert in the authors' suit found that the 8.2 million conversations had only 24 responses containing at least 30 matching words.
* Only 10 of the 212 books evaluated had any matches.

## 🔍 Case background

The filings were made as part of a legal battle brought by publishers and authors against Microsoft and OpenAI. The plaintiffs claim the companies built products on their works and now compete with them directly, in part by regurgitating copyrighted content. 

To streamline the process, the news publishers' and book authors' claims were consolidated under one judge, despite objections from the plaintiffs. In addition, the Trump administration filed a statement of interest in the New York Times case this week, supporting OpenAI. If the judge sides with the publishers and authors, the legal battle will continue in court. 

The New York Times, CIR, and the Authors Guild did not immediately respond to requests for comment.
