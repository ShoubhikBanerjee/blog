# OECD Study Finds Students Using AI Perform Worse Without Critical Assessment Training

According to newly released data from the OECD's Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), students who use artificial intelligence to help them study tend to perform worse at school than those who do not. The study, which collected data in 2025, represents the first time PISA has evaluated student performance since AI tools went truly mainstream.

## 📊 Scope of the study
The PISA study evaluated 15-year-old students across science, math, and reading. Key findings regarding student demographics and global trends include:
* **Global scale:** The data draws from over 760,000 students across 91 countries.
* **Socioeconomic factors:** After adjusting for socioeconomic status, students who never use AI generally outperformed those who do in science. AI use was found to be more common among students from relatively advantaged backgrounds, which likely reflects better access to hardware, stable internet access, and paid AI tools.
* **Geographic variation:** AI utilization varies widely by country. For example, over 95 percent of Vietnamese students reported using AI tools, compared to just 60 percent in Japan.
* **Curiosity connection:** The study noted that student curiosity levels correlate with AI use, peaking among daily users.

## ⚙️ Performance by AI task and frequency
The relationship between AI use and academic performance is complex, with results heavily dependent on how and how often the tools are used.

| Method of AI Use | Performance Impact |
| :--- | :--- |
| Summarizing texts or drafting writing assignments | Relatively worse performance |
| Preliminary research or broad "help me learn" use | Less of a performance drop |
| Weekly use to generally help learn | Outperformed non-users (though not by much) |
| Weekly general help + critical assessment training | All regular users outperformed non-users |

Frequency of use also plays a significant role in student outcomes:
* **Daily or yearly use:** Students who used AI daily, as well as those who only used it once or twice a year, tended to perform the worst.
* **Weekly or monthly use:** Better academic results were seen among monthly or weekly AI users.

## 💡 Intentional learning vs. shortcutting
The study's authors suggest that "moderate and intentional use of AI for schoolwork and learning" could have positive effects, especially when students are trained to critically assess the information they receive.

OECD director of learning and skills Andreas Schleicher explained the cognitive dynamics of using technology in education:

"In the same way that we do not become fit by watching sports but by doing sports, learning does not occur through the consumption of content, but as a productive cognitive struggle of the mind with new material," Schleicher wrote. "Where technology enables or enhances that cognitive struggle, students will advance."

Conversely, Schleicher added: "Where technology short-circuits the productive struggle of learning, it will undercut students’ development."
