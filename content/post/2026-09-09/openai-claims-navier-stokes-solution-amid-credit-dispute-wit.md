# OpenAI claims Navier–Stokes solution amid credit dispute with NYU and Anthropic

Today OpenAI announced that its agents have produced a proof that the full Navier–Stokes equations can break down, a result that would solve one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems.

## 🔍 Overview
- OpenAI announced agents solved Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem.
- The problem is one of seven Millennium Prize Problems selected by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000.
- Solutions carry a $1 million prize; before today only one other Millennium Problem had been solved.
- Navier–Stokes equations describe fluid flow and it was unknown whether they could break down to an impossible state such as infinite velocity.

## ⚙️ Technical Details
- The proof shows the full Navier–Stokes equations can break down.
- It was produced with an internal OpenAI model that outperforms the Astra model released the previous week.

### Model comparison
| Model | Note |
|-------|------|
| Astra | Released last week; considered impressive |
| Internal OpenAI model | Dramatically outperforms Astra; used for Navier–Stokes proof |

## ⚖️ Controversy
- Accusations that OpenAI used AI‑assisted work of NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic employee Levent Alpöge without credit.
- Buckmaster posted a proof of a simplified Navier–Stokes breakdown on Mastodon; he and Alpöge had worked for almost a year using publicly available models from OpenAI and Anthropic.
- OpenAI denied that its agents accessed Buckmaster and Alpöge’s transcripts or that employees trained on those transcripts; Mark Chen reiterated the denial.
- Buckmaster’s document describes two options presented by OpenAI employees: (1) let Buckmaster/Alpöge post their work and OpenAI post its solution the next day, or (2) collaborate on a paper that would exclude Alpöge because of his Anthropic affiliation.
- OpenAI also stated it does not plan to claim the million‑dollar prize.

## 💡 Why it matters
- Solving Navier–Stokes would settle a major open question in fluid dynamics and mathematics.
- The approach builds on work pioneered by mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez‑Zoroa, which experts consider promising.
- The dispute raises questions about attribution and transparency when AI models are trained on external contributions.
