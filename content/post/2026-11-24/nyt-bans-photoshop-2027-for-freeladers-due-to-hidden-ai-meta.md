# NYT bans Photoshop 2027 for freeladers due to hidden AI metadata

The New York Times is asking freelance photographers to stop using the newest version of Adobe Photoshop because the 2027 update includes AI features that may not be obvious to users.

## 📝 The Restriction
In an email seen by The Verge and posted on Threads, photography leaders Meaghan Looram and Josh Haner stated that the use of the new features "can result in metadata identifying photographs as AI-generated."

The specific directive reads: “At this time, we are requiring that all contributing photographers avoid using Adobe Photoshop 2027 (release 28.0.0 and subsequent updates) when working on assignment for The Times,”

## 🧩 Why the Metadata Matters
Adobe has spent the past few years integrating AI tools into its creative suite. The November update cited by the Times adds several new features, including:

*   **AI Assisted Editor view**: A single toolbar for accessing all Photoshop AI features.
*   **AI Assistant**: Provides step-by-step editing instructions.
*   **Prompt-based Tools**: For image transformation and editing.
*   **AI Upscaling Options**: For image enhancement.
*   **AI Recomposition**: A tool that recomposes images for different canvas sizes.

It is not always clear which tools are AI-powered, and some older options like automated selection tools may now be part of the AI suite. This ambiguity means photos can end up with metadata indicating they were edited with AI.

For the New York Times, which maintains that it does not “retouch or digitally manipulate” photos but only makes minimal color adjustments, this metadata is a risk. The organization views the presence of such tags as a loss of reader trust, as they serve as a blunt description of post-production work.

## ⚙️ Background and Context
In August, Adobe began rolling out support for C2PA metadata across its apps, which indicates whether an image is AI-generated or edited using AI features. The Times is issuing this restriction to prevent these tags from inadvertently appearing in its published photographs.

Neither Adobe nor the New York Times immediately responded to The Verge’s request for more information on which specific Photoshop features are causing the concern.
