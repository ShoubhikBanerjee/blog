# COP31 Climate Pact Fails Amid Rising Data Center Emissions

The United Nations climate conference, COP31, concluded in Antalya, Turkey, with global climate negotiations failing to pass a key pact due to disagreements over the growing carbon footprint of AI data centers. Despite the conference being the longest yet, delegates could not secure enough support for the deal, which the EU, UK, and several smaller vulnerable nations rejected as too weak.

## 📉 Negotiation Breakdown
Delegates ultimately failed to garner enough support to pass the two-week “pact” they had been bargaining on. The main points of contention included:
- The pact omitted the term “fossil fuels,” despite many nations calling for stronger commitments to phase out oil, coal, and gas.
- One of the most anticipated outcomes was a deal to raise electricity’s share of global energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035, compared to about 20 percent today. This target became harder to achieve as data centers caused electricity demand to skyrocket.
- The US, described as the epicenter of data center buildout and the biggest historical climate polluter, did not participate after the Trump administration pulled the country out of the Paris accord.

## 🏭 Industry Emissions Impact
Major technology companies are reporting increased environmental impacts while scaling AI infrastructure:
- Meta is “re-evaluating” its timeline to reach net zero emissions as it focuses on AI, reporting a 45 percent year-over-year increase in climate pollution.
- Google and Microsoft carbon footprints are also growing despite previous climate commitments.
- Data centers’ electricity consumption is forecast to double by 2030, according to the International Energy Agency.
- In the US, power sector emissions could increase 42 percent by 2030 as energy companies build more fossil fuel infrastructure to meet data center demand.
- Hyperscale data centers in the US currently create more climate pollution than the country of Denmark, according to Harvard researchers.

## ⚖️ Pledges and Controversies
Around 80 nations signed onto a voluntary pledge to increase electricity’s share of energy consumption to 35 percent over the next decade, though the list of participating countries has not yet been published. Another voluntary pledge to keep AI from derailing climate action by calling on data centers to run on less polluting energy failed to garner much support. Critics note that the pledge’s use of the term “low-emission energy” leaves the door open for natural gas to continue powering data centers.

Data centers are currently competing with other consumers for grid connections, finance, and electricity generation capacity, often outbidding more essential tasks like heating, air conditioning, and electrifying transportation.
