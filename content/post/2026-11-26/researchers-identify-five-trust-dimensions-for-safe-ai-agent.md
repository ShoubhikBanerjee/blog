# Researchers Identify Five Trust Dimensions for Safe AI Agents in Science

A new study involving 17 scientists from the natural, applied, and social sciences explores how to develop and trust AI agents capable of performing research tasks with varying degrees of autonomy. The work aims to ensure these tools expand scientific capability without compromising the ability to scrutinize evidence, verify methods, and stand behind findings.

## 🧩 How it works
The research draws on interviews to understand how scientists would steer AI development to reflect the responsibilities of their fields. Participants identified where they welcome agentic assistance, such as automating time-consuming tasks like processing large datasets, writing code, documenting experimental procedures, and handling administrative work. These automations could free researchers for fieldwork, mentoring, and developing new ideas, while also enabling the analysis of data volumes that are difficult to examine manually.

## ⚙️ Key details
One of the main findings concerns trust. Across the interviews, researchers identified five dimensions of scientific trust:

*   **Validation:** Checking whether the system overlooks conflicting evidence or makes claims the cited papers don’t support.
*   **Privacy:** Assuring that the system protects participants' privacy and limits who can access their information, especially when working with human-participant data.
*   **Objectivity:** Recognizing bias and ensuring tools do not retrieve only studies that support a favored hypothesis.
*   **Accountability:** Determining who is charged with verifying and standing behind AI-assisted findings.
*   **Transparency:** Understanding how an answer was reached, including which methods were used, which files were accessed, and whether results can be successfully reproduced.

Even a technically correct result may be unsuitable for scientific use if researchers cannot inspect how it was produced or if an agent accesses confidential data beyond what the researcher authorized. For instance, a researcher automating a lab protocol needs confidence that the system respects physical safety constraints, such as temperature limits or material toxicity.

| Dimension | Requirement | Example Concern |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Validation | Verify support from sources | Overlooking contradictory evidence or unsupported conclusions |
| Privacy | Protect sensitive info | Limiting access to human-participant data |
| Objectivity | Recognize bias | Tools retrieving only studies supporting a favored hypothesis |
| Accountability | Define verification responsibility | Determining who stands behind AI-assisted findings |
| Transparency | Understand process | Knowing which methods and files the agent accessed |

Another main finding suggests that scientists should help decide which research tasks agents take on, how those agents operate, and how their performance is evaluated. Interviewees proposed training in AI use and named developing and enforcing guidelines as a critical, timely challenge.

## 💡 Why it matters
Responsible science is about more than accuracy; scientific fields have ethical research norms because of past actions and choices by scientists and communities, and those norms cover how research is conducted and its impact. The interviews revealed a division between excitement about AI-driven progress and existential concern about the role of human scientists. To address this, the authors suggest participatory benchmarks. For example, a literature-review benchmark could test whether an agent overlooks contradictory evidence, while a coding benchmark could assess if an agent accesses files outside its authorized scope. These efforts aim to ensure that as AI agents follow the standards of responsible conduct of research, they do so with safeguards that allow scientists to trust them with their own studies.
