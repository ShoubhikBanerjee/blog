# Major US Infrastructure Outages Are Coincidence, Not Cyberattack

A recent series of outages affecting major US infrastructure providers, including Microsoft, the FAA, banks, United Airlines, an unnamed NSA network, and Apple, has raised suspicions of a coordinated cyberattack or conspiracy. Evidence indicates these incidents are unrelated and not the result of any cyberattack, nor are they caused by AI. The failures are attributed to a series of unfortunate coincidences involving old, incrementally patched systems often maintained by hobbyists without adequate backup systems.

## 🔍 Overview

It has been a difficult week for computerized infrastructure, with outages spanning critical sectors such as aviation, banking, and government. While the simultaneity of these events might suggest a common cause or conspiracy, the evidence clarifies that the incidents are distinct and unrelated. Notably, the failures are not the result of any cyberattack, coordinated or otherwise, and even more surprisingly, they do not seem to be the result of AI.

## ⚙️ Key Details

The underlying causes of these failures stem from the nature of the technology itself:
*   **Age and Maintenance:** Much of the infrastructure is old and has been incrementally patched and upgraded over decades.
*   **Volunteer Maintenance:** A surprising amount of the system is maintained by hobbyists without any recompense.
*   **Lack of Redundancy:** There often aren’t good backup systems.
*   **Centralization:** More of the infrastructure is centralized, meaning individual failures have cascading consequences across many different aspects of life and society.
*   **Brittleness:** Much of the tech infrastructure just barely works; when it fails, it can fail badly.

## 💡 Why It Matters

Security, safety, resilience, redundancy, and recoverability are essential parts of reliable technology, but they are costs that do not generate near-term profits. Modern shareholder capitalism, particularly private equity, prioritizes near-term profits, squeezing out apparent inefficiencies and positive intangibles. Market competition, which usually nudges companies to provide better services, is often removed in pursuit of these profits. From a quarterly budget perspective, spending on reliability measures is viewed as an inefficiency or waste. Consequently, costs of failure are often pushed onto society, leading to stranded travelers and inaccessible bank accounts. These economic pressures create brittle systems with single points of failure, making them more susceptible to cyberattacks and random mistakes. Fixing these issues requires regulation with enforcement and meaningful fines, as well as stringent antitrust enforcement.
