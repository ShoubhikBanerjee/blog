# Former DOJ Antitrust Chief Launches AI Startup FluxCap for Legal Research

Jonathan Kanter, who left the Justice Department’s Antitrust Division in 2024, has moved his fight against illegal monopolies into the private sector by co‑founding an AI‑powered legal‑tech startup.

## 🔍 Overview
FluxCap is a new company founded by former DOJ Antitrust Division chief Jonathan Kanter and former acting chief Doha Mekki. The team includes former DOJ staffers and ex‑Palantir engineers. Its first product is an AI‑based legal research and analysis platform aimed at helping plaintiffs build stronger antitrust cases.

## 🧩 How it works
- The platform combines proprietary technology with existing large‑language models.
- It is trained to recognise the factual and legal frameworks that determine a case’s success and to spot gaps in attorneys’ reasoning.
- It can sift through large volumes of discovery material, looking for signals that a company acted anticompetitively.
- The system was tested for months on real and hypothetical antitrust scenarios, including nuanced contexts like the Live Nation‑Ticketmaster phone call where a threat was inferred from a conversation.

## ⚙️ Key details
- **Founders**: Jonathan Kanter (former DOJ Antitrust head) and Doha Mekki (former acting Antitrust Division chief).
- **Team**: Former DOJ lawyers and Palantir engineers.
- **Focus**: Launching with antitrust cases; plans to expand to other practice areas.
- **First customers**: Antitrust boutique firm Kressin Powers; talks underway with public and private enforcers.
- **Resource impact**: Kanter says the tool provides “virtual capacity so that a small team can take on a large team,” delivering the manpower of roughly “20‑plus people working around the clock.”
- **DOJ context**: The Antitrust Division has a $245 million budget for FY 2026 and about 400 attorneys, which Kanter calls “just not realistic to have the resources necessary to take on these big companies.”

## 🚀 Availability
FluxCap is launching its platform with an emphasis on antitrust litigation. The company is positioning the tool as a “force multiplier” for watchdogs confronting powerful Big Tech firms and other monopolists.

## 💡 Why it matters
“Because it’s the only way that enforcement is going to work in the long term, is if technology and other tools are able to help scarce resources go significantly further than they can today,” Kanter tells The Verge. He adds that the effort is “existential,” reflecting the need for technology to extend limited legal resources against multibillion‑dollar corporations.
