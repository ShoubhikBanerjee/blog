Osmo launches first AI foundation model for smell

Osmo, an AI startup, announced that its new foundation model for smell has reached human‑level olfactory intelligence.

🔍 Overview

  • The model is described as "human-level olfactory intelligence," meaning it performs about as well as the average person at detecting and discriminating smells and about as well as a junior perfume designer at creating them.
  • Founder Alex Wiltschko says the goal is "to give computers a sense of smell."

🧩 How it works

  • A lab near Newark Airport in New Jersey employs around 30 experienced human sniffers who record descriptors such as "nutty," "peachy," and "marine."
  • Around 70 trained data labelers in the US and overseas also sniff samples and write standardized fragrance vocabulary notes.
  • Spectrometers and other chemical sensors run continuously to break down the molecular makeup of items ranging from fruit and flowers to pantry staples and organs.
  • The company has built a dataset of more than 10 million human labels and 800 million "scent tokens" that map molecules and concentrations to odor descriptions.
  • A smell‑printing robot the size of a school bus mixes ingredients to create samples for research and for paying customers, and it can generate scents on demand from a ChatGPT‑style text prompt.

⚙️ Key details

  • Prior to Osmo there were fewer than 200,000 human descriptions of smells in existing databases.
  • The model recently outperformed in‑house perfumers at one of the world’s largest consumer‑goods companies.
  • It is already being used by brands such as Procter & Gamble to design new retail fragrances.
  • The Gates Foundation invested $5 million at launch and later provided a $3.5 million grant; Osmo identified eight substances more repellent than DEET for disease‑carrying insects.
  • Partnerships include StockX, where smell analysis helps spot counterfeit sneakers.

🚀 Availability

  • The technology is offered to fragrance brands and other customers for custom scent design; a demo generated three scent options for a Portuguese custard‑tart brief aimed at 18‑23‑year‑old men, with a week‑long production timeline.

💡 Why it matters

  • The model demonstrates that large‑scale data collection and AI can approach human performance in a traditionally sensory domain.
  • Experts such as Michael Schmuker acknowledge the achievement, while critics like Avery Gilbert note that the company still relies heavily on human sensory panels in its patents.
