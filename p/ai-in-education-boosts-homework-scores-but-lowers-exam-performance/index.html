AI in education boosts homework scores but lowers exam performance

A large-scale study tracking 27,000 students in China found that generative AI improved homework efficiency but reduced exam performance over time.

🔍 Overview

After adopting generative AI, students’ homework scores rose by 18 percent, while the time spent on each assignment fell by about 30 percent, from 64 minutes to 45 minutes on average.

⚙️ Key details

  • The study, led by David Stromberg of Stockholm University, Victor Lei and Yanhui Wu of the University of Hong Kong, tracked students aged 12 to 18 for 30 months.
  • Data included homework, monthly closed-book exams, and high-stakes entrance exams across nine subjects.
  • When students sat monthly exams without AI, their scores fell by 20 percent within six months.
  • Performance in high-stakes entrance exams also declined, with the full effect emerging over a longer period.
  • The OECD’s Digital Education Outlook 2026 reached a similar conclusion.

📊 Student usage patterns

A survey by Ipsos for the Vodafone Foundation of 7,000 students aged 12 to 17 across seven European countries revealed the following uses of AI outside school:

Use CasePercentage of Students
Getting information56%
Having terms and concepts explained45%
Providing complete solutions to tasks31%
Using interactive or adaptive content29%
Creating personalised learning plans and tracking progress20%

🎓 Student perceptions

The HEPI/Kortext Student Generative AI Survey 2026, based on 1,054 full-time undergraduates in the UK, found:

  • 49 percent believe AI has improved their student experience.
  • 59 percent said AI made no difference.
  • 21 percent felt less lonely, while 20 percent felt more lonely.

🌍 Global adoption

China requires at least eight hours of AI instruction a year. In the Gulf, the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar have all begun integrating AI into their curricula.

💡 Why it matters

Research co-authored by Dragan Gasevic describes the potential erosion of productive cognitive effort as “metacognitive laziness.”
