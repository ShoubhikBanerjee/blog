OpenAI faces new criticism over possible use of unpublished math research

OpenAI’s recent announcement of a result on non‑sofic groups has drawn fresh criticism from mathematicians who say the company may have used unpublished research in its training data.

🔍 Overview

  • OpenAI announced a result involving non‑sofic groups, a class of infinite mathematical structures that cannot be approximated by finite ones.
  • The result was one of ten highlighted in the company’s announcement last month.

🧩 Concerns from the research community

  • Mathematician Andreas Thom posted on Mastodon that his earlier interactions with ChatGPT might have contributed to the success.
  • Thom and colleague Gábor Kun had prior work on non‑sofic groups that OpenAI acknowledged as heavily used.
  • Thom wrote to OpenAI researchers Sébastien Bubeck and Mark Sellke (a statistician at Harvard) asking whether his conversations were part of the training data; the reply only addressed direct access, not inclusion in the broader training corpus.
  • Thom said researchers cannot reverse‑engineer OpenAI’s training pipeline, and he urged the company to disclose datasets and usage terms.

⚙️ OpenAI’s response

  • The company amended its write‑up after criticism for not initially crediting Thom and Kun.
  • In a separate statement about its Millennium Prize Navier‑Stokes solution, OpenAI said it did not see any specific user data, but added that “while unlikely, we cannot rule out that de‑identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models.”
  • OpenAI has not commented to The Verge on the latest concerns.

💡 Why it matters

  • Thom called it “ethically indefensible” for a model to improve on non‑public research and then race the same researchers to publication without consent or credit.
  • Researchers fear such practices could push mathematics toward secrecy if rumors of breakthroughs trigger competitive races with well‑resourced AI firms.
