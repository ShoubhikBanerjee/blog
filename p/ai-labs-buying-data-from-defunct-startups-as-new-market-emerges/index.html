AI Labs Buying Data from Defunct Startups as New Market Emerges

AI labs are buying data from dead startups to train their models, turning the end‑of‑life ecosystem into a nascent data‑resale market.

🔍 Overview

  • When crypto startup founder Keelan Miskell wound down his company, he sold the code base for about $25,000 and a pending ~$15,000 for workspace data (Google, Notion, Figma, Slack) through SimpleClosure.
  • SimpleClosure added data sales to its services in April and now runs an Asset Hub platform that lets founders sell code and workspace data such as emails, chats, and documents.
  • Buyers include foundation AI labs, vendors that build simulated workspaces for AI training, and mature tech companies building their own models.

🧩 How the Market Works

  • SimpleClosure brokered over 150 deals and expects to return > $10 million to startups by the end of 2026.
  • 47 % of SimpleClosure’s deals now include code or data sales, according to CEO Dori Yona.
  • Rival Sunset also runs a data‑resale business; its CEO Brendan Mahony values assets by head count, years in operation, data volume, and tool usage, likening the exhaust data to “particle board.”
  • Demand surged last year as AI labs hit limits of public and synthetic datasets.
  • Companies like Surge AI buy archives to build reinforcement‑learning gyms where agents practice tasks (e.g., debugging IT architecture, drafting appellate briefs). Lead Nick Heiner says a rich Slack history can be more valuable than a combination of Slack, email, and calendars.

🤝 Notable Deals & Players

  • Google outbid Mercor for Spirit Airlines’ bankruptcy data: 100 M emails, 500 M Teams messages, 176 k employee records for $10 M (August).
  • SpaceX AI unit is reportedly weighing data purchases from struggling startups (September, Bloomberg).
  • Surge AI purchases real‑world archives to avoid synthetic‑world errors; pricing varies because valuations are based on research theses about task usefulness.

💡 Why It Matters

  • Attorneys like Greg Ewing call the data “legally radioactive,” noting that even after scrubbing, private details (doctor appointments, school pickups, date‑night plans) can remain.
  • Proceeds typically flow to senior creditors and investors, not to employees.
  • Law professor Ifeoma Ajunwa describes the practice as “captured capital” and argues that employers should obtain explicit consent before selling workers’ data, though no federal law currently bans such sales.
  • Founders have little visibility into who purchases their data or how it will be used.

The emerging ecosystem—coaches, “death doulas,” legal and tax specialists—reflects a growing market for the operational exhaust of companies that are winding down.
