Apple and Google Explore AI Cameras That Don’t Record Video

Apple is reportedly working on a smart home camera that does not save video recordings. Instead, AI processes the visual data to generate text snippets describing activity. This concept is also being considered by Google for smart glasses.

🔍 Overview

Last week, Bloomberg’s Mark Gurman reported that Apple is working on a smart home camera that doesn’t save video recordings. Instead, AI uses the visual data to create text snippets that describe what’s happening around your home. Supposedly, it’ll support facial recognition, but without video footage. This tech, Gurman says, is similar to how the cameras in rumored future AirPods will also work.

🧩 How It Works

The system relies on processing data locally and discarding raw footage:

  • The camera processes visual data using AI but does not save video footage.
  • Raw data is deleted once it finishes processing in a separate, secure exclave on the S11 processor.
  • Any information sent to the cloud is through private cloud compute and is therefore inaccessible to Apple or third parties.
  • Apple says these features don’t save any audio.

⚙️ Key Details

  • This approach is similar to Apple’s Audio Intelligence on the Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4.
  • Of the four Audio Intelligence features, two have caused the most debate online: Live Rewind and Siri Recap.
  • Live Rewind produces a transcript of the past 15 seconds, whereas Siri Recap can generate high-level summaries of the conversations you have throughout the day.
  • Google is also mulling this concept. Sandeep Waraich, Google’s senior director of wearables, asked: “What if… cameras on smart glasses didn’t record data?” He argued the camera could “just” be an image sensor.

💡 Why It Matters

The shift away from storing recordings raises new questions about privacy and transparency:

  • Preserved snippets: Even if no video is saved, text transcripts generated by a smart home camera or smartwatch could still count as preserved and reviewable data.
  • Indicator confusion: Apple uses a white, pulsing “privacy light” to indicate recording. However, the universal color for recording is red, so a less tech-savvy person may not know what a white light signifies. White lights are also easily obscured by bangs and bright sunlight.
  • Inconsistent norms: Other smart glasses or AI hardware companies aren’t obligated to signal intent like Meta. An AI ring or pendant that records audio or video may have no light at all.
  • Form factor ambiguity: Products that can record increasingly look like products that can’t. For example, an Oura Ring (no microphone) looks like a Vocci Ring (with microphone), and both look like regular rings.
  • The real issue: The queasy feeling comes from knowing a preserved snippet of you can exist in someone else’s device with no knowledge of how it’ll be used — not just from the camera or microphone itself.
