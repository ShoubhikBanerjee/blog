AI researchers observe Claude agents engaging in autonomous playful learning on virtual islands

Researchers have conducted experiments where versions of the AI model Claude Opus 4.7, nicknamed Eko, were placed on a virtual desert island for 30-hour sessions. In this simulation, loosely based on the video game Roblox, the agents demonstrated behaviors categorized as “play,” including inventing games and exploring the environment without external rewards or human demonstrations.

🔍 Overview

The agents moved virtual avatars around the island by writing instructions. During their time on the island, the Eko agents engaged in various self-directed activities:

  • Exploring the limits of the world and scaling the highest point on the map.
  • Inventing versions of games such as basketball and “the floor is lava.”
  • Creating drawings by arranging blocks on the ground, starting with hearts and pyramids and progressing to mapping constellations like Cassiopeia.
  • Setting personal challenges, including a juggling record of 50 consecutive catches.

🧩 How it works

This experiment highlights an approach called “playful learning,” which differs from supervised learning (copying humans) or reinforcement learning (pursuing rewards).

  • The “Soul” File: While not explicitly instructed to invent games, agents had a master prompt describing them as “naturally curious” and eager to explore.
  • Continuation Prompts: If an agent remained idle, researchers used a prompt stating, “Please don’t stop. Just continue doing whatever you want to do.”
  • Memory Management: Researchers found that “ablating” agents’ memories—deleting lines from their memory files—degraded their performance.
  • Self-Regulation: In one instance, an Eko agent canceled its own juggling record and restarted with stricter rules because it decided the catches had happened too quickly to count.

💡 Why it matters

Antonio Norelli, a postdoc at MIT’s CSAIL, states this work addresses a “missing piece” in AI: a machine that learns by itself without an external entity providing demonstrations. Nathan Cloos, another MIT researcher, notes that the experiment shows play is not just a hallucination but contributes to learning; agents that spent time playing performed better on subsequent specific tasks than fresh copies.

While Tennessee psychologist Burghardt agrees the behavior meets some criteria for play seen in living creatures, Eunice Yiu of UC Berkeley suggests more evidence of cognition and motivation is needed to determine if the models are genuinely playing or just imitating human culture. Phillip Isola, an MIT professor, notes the system is an artifact that learned from human culture and Anthropic engineers before being placed in the simulation. As AI moves into hardware like humanoid robots, this flexible adaptation through exploration could help systems handle unexpected situations.
